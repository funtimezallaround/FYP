\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chp:conclusion}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This project demonstrates that composing open-source foundation models into a domain-specific
pipeline can extract positional and kinematic metrics from handheld poolside video without
bespoke large-scale training data collection. The central \gls{ai} contribution is the
\gls{mitl} annotation pipeline, which leverages \gls{sam}~2~\cite{ravi2024sam} and
statistical filtering to generate pseudo-labelled training data, enabling a \gls{yolo26}s
detector~\cite{jocher_ultralytics_2023} to be fine-tuned for an underwater domain in which no
prior labelled dataset existed. Applied to a single 50\,m breaststroke sequence, the resulting
system recovered 18 of 20 spatial checkpoints with an \gls{mae} of 1.005\,s and an \gls{rmse} of 1.210\,s
(Table~\ref{tab:split_errors_updated}). At the macro level, stroke-phase symmetry is
recoverable from centroid-relative signals, while sub-stroke wrist kinematics, degraded by model
domain shift, are not yet reliable. The system establishes a feasibility baseline for
foundation-model composition in a visually challenging domain where labelled data is scarce,
rather than a production-ready coaching tool.

\section{Critical Reflection: Limitations and Trade-offs}

The \gls{mitl} pipeline's core strength is its ability to bootstrap a task-specific detector from
unlabelled domain footage. Its central limitation follows from the same mechanism:
pseudo-labels generated from a single pool encode the visual statistics of that environment,
and the fine-tuned detector inherits this specificity. Marker confidence jitter on
out-of-distribution footage (Section~\ref{sec:marker_detection}) illustrates the resulting
fragility: even within the same physical pool, changes in lighting and turbidity introduce a
domain shift sufficient to destabilize the scale estimate. A pseudo-labelling flywheel seeded
across multiple environments would directly address this, but the present evaluation cannot
quantify the performance boundary, and the two unrecoverable N/A splits in
Table~\ref{tab:split_errors_updated} are a concrete symptom of this model specificity.

ViTPose~\cite{xu2022vitpose}, pre-trained on the terrestrial \gls{coco}
dataset~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, was selected for its strong general-purpose pose estimation
performance. In the aquatic domain, this choice introduces a systematic distribution gap:
caustic lighting patterns, chromatic aberration, surface occlusion, and motion blur from
limited exposure are collectively absent from \gls{coco}'s training distribution. The observable
consequences are confidence collapse and keypoint hallucination at distal joints, both visible
in the wrist velocity artefacts reported in Section~\ref{sec:kinematic_outputs}. Refraction
further introduces a systematic lateral bias in submerged keypoint positions that a
rectilinear camera model cannot correct without depth and angle-of-incidence information.
These failure modes reflect a principled limit on how far a terrestrial foundation model can
generalize under optical domain shift without adaptation. Taken together with the unknown
timing uncertainty inherent in single-observer ground truth, the reported \gls{mae} of 1.005\,s is
best understood as a lower bound on real-world deployment error rather than a performance
ceiling.

Finally, the system's reliance on a single monocular video stream introduces the unavoidable assumption that the swimmer's motion is approximately parallel to the camera plane. This is a common constraint in single-camera sports analytics, but it limits the system's applicability to scenarios where the swimmer's trajectory deviates significantly from this plane, such as during turns or in open water. The use of lane rope markers as calibration objects partially mitigates this by providing reference points for scale estimation, but it does not address the fundamental issue of depth ambiguity inherent in monocular vision. 

\section{Ethical and Practical Considerations}

\gls{ai}-based analysis of athletes raises immediate questions of privacy and consent. In this
project, participation was governed by an institutional consent process, whereas a deployed system
would require explicit policies covering data retention periods, access controls, and
athletes' rights to withdraw their data and request its deletion, none of which are determined
by the model architecture or training pipeline. In this project, all data collection and processing was conducted with the informed consent of the participant.

Positional and kinematic data generated by an \gls{ai} pipeline from athlete footage constitute
performance intelligence with potential commercial value, relevant to talent identification,
training optimization, and competitive scouting. Whether such data belongs to the athlete, the
coach, the club, or the technology provider is not resolved by any technical design decision, and
it requires explicit governance frameworks established before deployment.

A further concern specific to \gls{ai}-generated metrics is the risk of over-reliance. A system
that produces numerical outputs such as split times and velocity profiles may lead users to
treat those figures as ground truth rather than as estimates produced by models with the
distributional limitations described in Chapter~\ref{chp:evaluation}. A deployable system
should surface confidence intervals alongside point predictions, making model uncertainty
legible to non-technical users and mitigating the risk of systematic over-interpretation.

\section{Future Work}

The most impactful near-term improvement to keypoint quality would be fine-tuning
ViTPose~\cite{xu2022vitpose} on an aquatic-domain dataset. The \gls{mitl} pipeline developed in
this project provides a natural mechanism for generating pseudo-labelled pose annotations
across diverse pool environments at scale, creating a self-reinforcing data flywheel that
progressively reduces the \gls{coco} distribution gap~\cite{lin2014microsoft}. A domain-adapted
pose model would directly address confidence collapse and keypoint hallucination at distal
joints, and would lay the groundwork for the sub-stroke technique analysis that the current
system cannot reliably support.

The current pipeline treats each frame largely independently, delegating inter-frame coherence
to the Kalman filter~\cite{kalman1960new}. Replacing the detection and filtering stack with a
temporal transformer architecture, such as a video-based extension of \gls{sam}~2~\cite{ravi2024sam}
or a transformer-based multi-object tracker, would allow the model to reason over a learned
temporal context window. This architectural change could recover occluded marker positions and
keypoints more robustly than the present carry-forward heuristic, and would reduce sensitivity
to rapid camera motion without relying on hand-crafted filtering parameters.

Fusing the video-based trajectory with readings from a waterproof \gls{imu}
worn by the swimmer would introduce a sensing modality that is immune to optical domain shift.
\gls{imu} acceleration data could serve as an additional observation in the Kalman filter process
model, constraining the position estimate during marker-loss intervals and providing a
physically grounded check on the scale carry-forward mechanism that is the primary source of
non-linear drift in the current system.

To address the planar motion assumption, a multi-camera setup could be employed to triangulate 3D positions, or a depth-sensing camera could be used to directly capture the swimmer's spatial coordinates. However, these hardware changes would increase system complexity and cost, and they would require additional calibration procedures. An alternative software-based approach could involve training a depth estimation model on the aquatic domain, using the lane rope markers as reference points for scale and depth cues. This would allow for approximate 3D localization from a single camera, albeit with reduced accuracy compared to multi-camera triangulation.

Finally, realizing the system's practical potential requires adapting it for real-time
inference. This entails profiling and optimizing the \gls{yolo26}s~\cite{jocher_ultralytics_2023}
and ViTPose inference graphs for \gls{gpu} or \gls{npu} execution, and developing an interface that
presents model outputs alongside calibrated uncertainty estimates. Consistent with the
considerations raised in Section~\ref{chp:conclusion}.3, surfacing uncertainty is not a
cosmetic addition but a structural requirement for responsible deployment of \gls{ai}-generated
performance metrics.
